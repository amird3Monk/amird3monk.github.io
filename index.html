Selected project

Slider-bed belt conveyor

A unit-load conveyor for boxes and totes: 3 m between pulley centres, 400 mm belt, 800 mm to the belt surface, 150 kg of live load, driven from the discharge end. I took it from a written brief through drive selection, shaft and key design, component selection from catalogues, a full 3D assembly and detail drawings.

The parts below are the decisions I would defend in an interview. Each one came from a calculation or a constraint, not from a preference.

Shaft design

Sized by deflection, not stress

The belt supplier limits pulley shaft deflection to y ≤ 0.001·b₀, which at a 400 mm belt is 0.40 mm. That limit, not bending stress, is what sets the shaft diameter. A plain Ø30 shaft passes every stress check and still fails the belt — it would track off the pulley.

Ø35 → 0.261 mm · Ø30 → 0.484 mm · limit 0.40 mm

Drive · friction

The belt is the torque fuse

Drive is by friction over a 180° wrap, so the capstan equation sets the ceiling: T₁/T₂ ≤ e^(μθ). With μ = 0.15 on a bare steel drum that is 1.60, and the screw take-up must hold enough slack tension to stay inside it. It also means the belt slips at about 56 N·m — well below the 195 N·m the gearbox can deliver, so the drivetrain protects itself.

T₂ required 748 N · take-up gives 1039 N · margin 1.39

Structure

A bearing flange chose the section

The frame is PFC 180 and not PFC 150 for one reason: the flanged bearing unit needs at least 108 mm of flat web to bolt to. PFC 180 offers 135 mm of clear web, PFC 150 only about 100. The bought component set the structural section, which is usually the way round it happens.

Flange 108 × 108 · clear web 135 · bolt pitch 82.5

Detail design

A 12 mm radius moved a plate

A clash check found the take-up plate overlapping both frames by 45.9 mm³. Not a modelling slip — the channel's root radius means the clear flat web stops at Y ±62.5, so a 130 mm plate was resting on the fillet instead of flat steel. In the workshop that is a plate that rocks, with a gap under the weld. Reducing it to 120 mm clears the radius by 2.5 mm and keeps 13.25 mm of edge distance at the bolt slots.

Hand integration of the sliver: 45.9 mm³ — matched to 3 figures

Drawing practice

The crown is given as diameters

A crowned pulley keeps the belt tracking. Writing “crown 0.4” invites half a workshop to read a radius and half a diameter. The drawing instead states three diameters at three stated stations, so the ambiguity cannot survive — and the crown is turned in the same setup as the outside diameter.

Ø100.00 over the central 150 band · Ø99.20 at each face edge

Manufacture

Machine after welding, in one setup

The pulley is a weldment: tube, two end discs, two hubs. Bore the hubs first and weld after, and the two bores are no longer coaxial, the drum will not slide onto the shaft, and the face runs out of true. Boring and skimming after welding, between centres, fixes all three because bore and OD are cut on the same axis. The 0.8 mm per side skim allowance exists to machine the distortion out.

Ø35 H7 bore · 0.05 TIR face runout · Ra 1.6 bore

Hands on

Workshop and manufacturing

I trained as a graduate trainee at BITAC in 2022, working on centre lathes and milling machines, and did turning and milling through my BSc laboratories. That is the part of engineering I most want to teach, because it is where drawings stop being abstract.

Design work and machining answer each other. Knowing that a bore and an outside diameter should be cut in one setup, or why a keyway is dimensioned from the opposite bore wall so an inspector can reach it with a slip gauge, comes from having stood at the machine.

Teaching

Explaining is the job

I have tutored GCSE Maths one to one since 2025 and did peer tutoring during my MSc at Kingston. I hold a standard DBS, and an enhanced DBS including the Children's Barred List is in progress.

The conveyor above is also a teaching portfolio. Every decision on it is written down with the reasoning attached — why the shaft steps up, why the section was chosen by a bought component, why a crown is dimensioned one way and not another. A student can follow that; a finished model alone teaches nothing.

I am looking for lecturing and instructor work in machining and manufacturing, and I am interested in a PhD route alongside it.
